## What is a project?

A **project** is *a temporary endeavour undertaken to create a unique product, service, or
result*. The deck breaks that into three properties: **temporary** (it has a beginning and an
end), **unique** (it produces a specific outcome), and **purposeful** (it is created to achieve
an objective).

**Temporary does not mean short.** A project is temporary because it *ends*, not because it is
brief. The deck's own examples: a student event runs 2 weeks, a mobile application 6 months, a
new warehouse 2 years, a railway system 10+ years — all projects.

A project ends when **the objective is achieved**, when **the project is no longer needed**,
when **the objective cannot be achieved**, or when **the sponsor decides to terminate it**. It
does not continue indefinitely; if something runs forever with no end date, it is an ongoing
business activity, not a project.

**Unique does not mean "nobody has ever done this before."** Uniqueness is a *unique
combination* of objective, customer, location, requirements, resources and context. Many hotels
already have mobile apps; implementing one for *this particular hotel* is still a unique
project. The older deck makes the same point with data: of thousands of patents analysed, 95%
of problems already had known solutions and only 5% were truly novel — projects are defined by
their **unique purpose**, not by unprecedented technology.

The older deck's working definition adds detail: a project is *a complex, non-routine, one-time
effort to produce a unique product or service limited by time, budget, resource and
specifications*, with a defined lifespan, a well-defined objective, people from several
disciplines, a life cycle, and specific time/cost/performance requirements.

## Projects vs. operations

The single sentence to memorise: **operations RUN the business; projects CHANGE the business.**
Checking in guests, cleaning rooms, serving food, processing bookings and managing daily
inventory are operations. Developing the hotel app, installing a new booking system, renovating
the lobby, implementing a smart-energy system and redesigning the guest process are projects.

| | Project | Operation |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | Temporary | Ongoing |
| Output | Unique | Repetitive |
| Purpose | Change | Sustain |
| Team | Often temporary | Usually stable |
| End | Objective achieved | Continues |

The older deck adds two rows. **People:** projects use dynamic, temporary teams *not* aligned
with the organisational structure, operations use functional teams that are. **Authority of the
manager:** in operations it is generally formal, direct line authority; for a project manager it
varies by organisational structure and is generally minimal, if any. Both share the key
similarities — planned, executed and controlled, performed by people, resource-constrained.

Not every improvement is routine operations: a business problem (poor inventory visibility) →
an improvement idea (we need real-time information) → a **project** (implement an
inventory-management system).

## From strategy to an approved project

Organisations start projects for four reasons: a **problem** (something is not working), an
**opportunity** (something could create value), a **requirement** (something must change), or
**strategy** (the organisation wants to move in a new direction).

Projects translate strategy into action along this chain:
**Strategy** (where do we want to go?) → **Objectives** (what must we achieve?) →
**Initiatives** (what could we change?) → **Projects** (what will we implement?) →
**Deliverables** (what will the project create?) → **Benefits** (what value should result?).

But budget, people, time, technology and management attention are all limited, so not every
good idea can be funded. **Project selection** is *the process of evaluating potential projects
and deciding which projects should receive organisational resources*. It answers three
questions: **Should we?** (strategic alignment) · **Can we?** (feasibility) · **Is it worth it?**
(value versus cost and risk).

A **project scoring model** makes that comparison explicit: score each criterion 1 (low) to
5 (high) and compute **Weighted Score = Σ (Weight × Score)**. The deck's criteria are Strategic
Alignment 30%, Expected Benefits / Customer-Business Value 25%, Feasibility, Cost, and Risk.

## Business case vs. project selection

A **business case** answers *"Why should the organisation invest in this project?"* — it covers
**business need, strategic alignment, expected benefits, estimated costs/resources, major risks
and feasibility**, and recommends Proceed / Revise / Reject.

> **Exam focus.** These two are the classic adjacent-term trap. A **business case** asks
> *"Is this project worth doing?"* — **one project**, focused on need, benefits, cost, risks.
> **Project selection** asks *"Which projects should we do?"* — **several projects**, focused on
> comparing, prioritising and allocating limited resources.

An idea is not an approved project. The sequence is **Idea** (high-level, often incomplete) →
**Business Case** (structured justification) → **Evaluation** (score against agreed criteria,
compare with other ideas) → **Decision** (approve / defer / reject, confirm funding and scope)
→ **Approved Project** (formally authorised and funded, **project charter created**, planning
and execution begin). A project should exist only because there is sufficient justification for
investing in it.

## The project life cycle

The life cycle describes **where the project is in its journey**. The deck's level-of-effort
curve shows four overlapping phases:

| Phase | Work |
|---|---|
| **Defining** | Goals, specifications, tasks, responsibilities |
| **Planning** | Schedules, budgets, resources, risks, staffing |
| **Executing** | Status reports, changes, quality, forecasts |
| **Closing** | Train customer, transfer documents, release resources, evaluation, lessons learned |

Effort is low at the start, peaks during Executing, and tails off in Closing.

Three characteristics run across the cycle (Initiation → Planning → Execution → Closing):
**uncertainty is high at the beginning and falls**, **knowledge increases through the project**,
and the **cost of change increases over time**. The lesson: **early decisions matter, because
later changes are usually more expensive.**

## PMBOK 6: five process groups

PMBOK 6 is organised around **two** structures: **5 process groups** (what project managers need
to *do*) and **10 knowledge areas** (what they need to *manage*).

| # | Process group | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Initiating** | Define/authorise a new project or phase |
| 2 | **Planning** | Define how the work will be done |
| 3 | **Executing** | Perform the work |
| 4 | **Monitoring & Controlling** | Track performance, compare and adjust |
| 5 | **Closing** | Complete the project or phase |

Monitoring & Controlling is drawn *wrapping around* Planning and Executing with two-way arrows
— it runs continuously alongside them, it is not a stage that comes after Executing.

> **Exam focus — life cycle ≠ process groups.** The deck devotes a whole slide to this. The
> **project life cycle** describes *where the project is in its journey* (e.g. Concept → Design
> → Build → Launch, or Defining → Planning → Executing → Closing). The **process groups**
> describe *what project-management activities are being performed* — and they repeat inside
> every phase of the life cycle. "Concept / Design / Build / Launch" is a life cycle, not a set
> of process groups.

## PMBOK 6: the ten knowledge areas

> **Exam focus.** "Which knowledge area of project management is this?" is a named exam
> question. Learn the list in order and learn one give-away cue for each.

| # | Knowledge area | Cue in a scenario |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Integration Management** | Pulling everything together: charter, the single project management plan, integrated change control, trade-offs across areas |
| 2 | **Scope Management** | What is in and what is out; requirements, WBS, preventing scope creep |
| 3 | **Schedule Management** | Activities, sequence, durations, critical path, dates |
| 4 | **Cost Management** | Estimating, budgeting, spend against budget |
| 5 | **Quality Management** | Acceptance criteria, standards, testing, defects |
| 6 | **Resource Management** | *Internal* people and equipment: acquiring, developing and managing the team |
| 7 | **Communications Management** | Who gets what information, how, how often — reports, meetings |
| 8 | **Risk Management** | Uncertain future events: identify, analyse, plan responses, monitor |
| 9 | **Procurement Management** | *External* suppliers: contracts, vendors, purchasing |
| 10 | **Stakeholder Management** | Identifying, analysing and engaging the people affected |

Two traps to rehearse. **Resource vs. Procurement**: getting people from inside the organisation
is Resource; contracting an outside vendor is Procurement. **Communications vs. Stakeholder**:
deciding *who is affected and how to win them over* is Stakeholder; deciding *what information
goes to whom, in what format and how often* is Communications.

Note the naming. The newer deck lists **Schedule Management** (3) and **Resource Management**
(6), which are the PMBOK 6 names. The older deck's list still uses the earlier edition's
**Time Management** and **Human Resource Management** for the same two areas — same content,
older labels.

The course itself maps onto this: Week 4 Foundations → Integration; Week 5 Initiation & Scope →
Scope and Stakeholder; Week 6 Planning → Schedule, Resource, Cost; Week 7 Supporting Plans →
Risk, Communications, Quality; Weeks 9/10/14 apply all areas.

## Constraints, trade-offs and success

The **triple constraint** is **Scope** (what are we delivering?), **Schedule** (when?) and
**Cost** (how much?), with **Quality / Value** at the centre. **Resources** and **Risk** are the
other important constraints. The older deck adds the governance point: **the priority among
these constraints is decided by the project sponsors.**

Constraints trade off against each other. "Add AI recommendations to the hotel app" means scope
↑, which usually means time ↑ and/or cost ↑. "Can we launch two months earlier?" means time ↓,
which requires cost ↑ (more people, overtime, faster tools) or scope ↓ (remove features). The
older deck names the three chronic pressures: **scope creep** (uncontrolled expansion of scope
*without* adjusting time, cost and resources), **schedule compression** (shorten the duration
without reducing scope) and **budget compression** (cut the budget without reducing scope).

**Project success** has two layers. The **traditional view** is on scope, on time, on budget.
The **business view** asks whether benefits were realised, stakeholders satisfied, strategic
objectives supported and the deliverable actually used. *Project delivery is not the same as
business success: a successful project delivers the outputs; a successful investment delivers
outcomes and value.* A system delivered on time and on budget that nobody uses has failed in
business terms.

## Roles: who is responsible?

| Role | Question it owns | Responsibilities |
|---|---|---|
| **Sponsor** | WHY / SHOULD | Owns the business justification, provides support and resources, makes major decisions |
| **Project Manager** | HOW | Integrates the project work, coordinates the team, manages constraints, communicates and resolves issues |
| **Project Team** | DO | Performs the project work, creates the deliverables |

The project manager is **the integrator**: the hub that keeps scope, schedule, cost, quality,
resources, communications, risk, procurement and stakeholders aligned with each other. The
older deck's formal definition: *the person assigned by the performing organisation to lead the
team that is responsible for achieving the project objectives* — one title, many roles
(organiser, facilitator, persuader, problem solver, coach).

## The running cases

**Hotel Digital Transformation.** A boutique hotel wants to improve guest experience, increase
operational efficiency and reduce dependence on OTAs. The proposed solution is a **digital guest
service platform**: direct booking, mobile check-in/out, in-room ordering and guest feedback.
Its business case: need = high OTA commissions and inefficient guest-service processes;
benefits = more direct bookings, faster guest service, higher in-room sales, better customer
data; cost = **฿3 million**; risks = low adoption, integration problems, staff resistance,
cybersecurity; decision = Proceed / Revise / Reject.

**FreshMart.** 25 urban supermarkets. Online orders up **30%**, average delivery time up from
**2 to 4 hours**, inventory accuracy only **88%**, customers complaining about out-of-stocks and
late deliveries. Strategy for the next two years: *"provide a faster and more reliable
omnichannel shopping experience."* Maximum investment budget **฿8 million**. Four candidate
projects:

| | Project | Investment | Duration | Primary impact |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Real-Time Inventory System | ฿6m | 9 months | Inventory accuracy 88% → 97% |
| B | New Urban Fulfillment Center | ฿8m | 12 months | Delivery time 4 hrs → 2 hrs |
| C | AI Demand Forecasting | ฿4m | 6 months | Demand accuracy, inventory efficiency |
| D | Customer Mobile App Upgrade | ฿3m | 5 months | Customer experience, online sales |

They are scored on strategic alignment, expected benefits, cost, feasibility and risk — B scores
highest on alignment and benefits but is the most expensive and consumes the whole budget; D is
cheapest but does not directly solve the inventory problem.
