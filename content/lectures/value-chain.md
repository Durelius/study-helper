## What "value" actually means

The deck opens by refusing the obvious definition. **Value** is *not solely the cost of
production*; it is the **perceived benefits that the customer is willing to pay for**.
That shift matters, because it makes value something you can raise without touching
cost at all.

From this comes the deck's core identity:

> **Value Created − Cost of Creating Value = Margin**

The arrows on the slide point in three directions: push **Value Created up**, push
**Cost of Creating Value down**, and **Margin** rises. Every later part of the framework
is a place to do one of those two things.

## Margin as a formula

The deck then writes margin out over a product line:

> Margin = Σ (P_i × N_sold_i) − Σ (C_i × N_sold_i) = Σ (P_i − C_i) × N_sold_i

where `P_i` is the price of product *i*, `C_i` its unit cost and `N_sold_i` the quantity
sold. The point of writing it this way is the rhetorical question next to it: *why
doesn't Pop Mart simply raise prices to capture the full margin seen in the resale
market?* Because margin depends on **both** the per-unit spread `(P − C)` **and** the
volume `N_sold` — and the supply/demand diagram beside the formula shows that raising
price moves you off the equilibrium and sells fewer units. A price rise can shrink
total margin.

## Value chain vs supply chain vs project management

**Value chain**: the full range of activities a company undertakes — the deck adds the
gloss **(Internal Operations)** — to bring a product or service *from its initial
conception to its end use and beyond*. It is **inward looking** and is called an
**umbrella concept**: it identifies opportunities for improvement and innovation across
all business functions.

**Supply chain**: the deck labels the row *Suppliers → Company → Distributors → Users*
as the supply chain, i.e. **External Logistics**. It is **outward looking**, it is
**a part of the broader value chain**, and it is the **external flow of goods, materials
and information** from suppliers through the company to the customer — focused on
logistics and sourcing. Its job is to *move stuff efficiently*.

**Project management**: *makes change happen on time and on budget*. It executes
specific improvements or innovations, and is explicitly **a tool used to improve the
value chain or supply chain through structured initiatives** — not a rival framework.

| Concept | Direction | One-line job |
|---|---|---|
| Value chain | Inward looking (umbrella concept) | Adds value at each step |
| Supply chain | Outward looking (part of the value chain) | Moves stuff efficiently |
| Project management | Delivery vehicle | Makes change happen on time and on budget |

## Porter's value chain: the frame

**Porter's Value Chain** is *a framework to map out internal activities that add value
to your customers*. The diagram is a large arrow: four **support activities** stacked
as horizontal bands across the top (Firm Infrastructure, Human Resource Management,
Technology Development, Procurement), **five primary activities** as vertical columns
along the bottom (Inbound Logistics → Operations → Outbound Logistics → Marketing &
Sales → Service), and **Margin** as the arrowhead on the right — the residual that the
whole configuration produces.

**Primary activities** directly develop your inputs into outputs, **happen in order**,
and are the **source of cost advantage**. **Support activities** help make the primary
activities more efficient, have **no order**, and are the **source of differentiation
advantage**. Getting that pairing the right way round is the single most examinable
sentence on the slide.

`[slides 10, 11]`

## The five primary activities

| Primary activity | What it covers | Deck example |
|---|---|---|
| **Inbound Logistics** | Receiving, storing and distributing **inputs** | Raw material delivery for a Thai food business |
| **Operations** | **Transforming** inputs into finished products or services | Cooking meals, manufacturing phones |
| **Outbound Logistics** | **Delivering** the product to customers | Food delivery service, shipping to retailers |
| **Marketing & Sales** | Creating awareness and **persuading** customers | Social media ads, in-store promotions |
| **Service** | Supporting the customer **after the sale** | Product warranty, tech support, loyalty programs |

## The four support activities

| Support activity | What it covers | Deck example |
|---|---|---|
| **Firm Infrastructure** | Company structure, finance, legal, strategy | Accounting system, office setup |
| **Human Resource Management** | Hiring, training, retaining people | Employee onboarding, team training |
| **Technology Development** | Innovation, R&D, tech tools to improve the process | POS systems, food ordering apps |
| **Procurement** | **Acquiring** resources such as raw materials or tools | Sourcing fresh ingredients, finding suppliers |

## The two traps you will be tested on

> **Exam focus.** The case-study question hands you a list of concrete business
> activities and asks which box each one belongs in. Two boundaries decide most marks.

**Procurement (support) vs Inbound Logistics (primary).** Procurement is the *deciding
and acquiring*: negotiating the contract, choosing the supplier, buying the IP or the
machine. Inbound Logistics is the *physical handling once it is yours*: receiving,
storing, moving inputs to where they are used. "Signs an ethical sourcing agreement with
a coffee cooperative" is Procurement; "warehouses the imported beans and runs cold-chain
to the stores" is Inbound Logistics.

**Technology Development (support) vs Operations (primary).** Technology Development is
*building the tool* — the app, the analytics platform, the R&D. Operations is *running
the transformation* using whatever tools exist. "Builds an AI inventory dashboard" is
Technology Development; "brews the drinks to the standard SOP" is Operations. Note the
deck's own wrinkle: for **Pop Mart** it files **3D design & prototyping and QC under
Operations**, because that *is* the firm's transformation step, while its Technology
Development is customer data analytics and digital commerce tech.

A third, milder trap: **Marketing & Sales vs Service**. Persuading someone to buy is
Marketing & Sales; everything that supports them once they have bought — warranty,
loyalty redemption, hotline — is Service.

## Worked example: Starbucks (Thailand)

Entered Thailand in **1998**; **574 stores in 2026**, with **65% of all stores
concentrated in Bangkok**.

| Activity | Starbucks Thailand |
|---|---|
| Inbound Logistics | Warehousing imported coffee beans; receiving fresh local milk, pastries and packaging; cold-chain logistics to stores |
| Operations | Brewing and preparing beverages in stores; localized store design and layout; quality control and barista SOPs |
| Outbound Logistics | Customer pickup in-store; mobile ordering (Starbucks App); delivery via GrabFood, LINE MAN |
| Marketing & Sales | Seasonal Thai campaigns (e.g. Thai Tea Frappe); promotions via Starbucks Rewards; localized branding and cup designs |
| Service | Mobile payment (LINE Pay, TrueMoney); loyalty points and digital coupons; customer support via app and hotline |
| Firm Infrastructure | Operated by Coffee Concepts Thailand; strategic location planning in malls and airports; finance, legal and franchise operations management |
| Human Resource Management | Barista training & certification; partner (employee) rewards and growth plans; localized service culture |
| Technology Development | Starbucks Thailand mobile app; digital ordering, AI-based inventory; store operations dashboard |
| Procurement | Ethical global coffee sourcing; contracts with Thai bakery and merchandise suppliers; sourcing store materials and tech equipment |

## Worked example: Pop Mart

Chinese pop-culture and designer-toy company **founded in 2010**; **Molly**, its first
designer art toy, in **2016**; **IPO in 2020**.

| Activity | Pop Mart |
|---|---|
| Inbound Logistics (physical flow) | Receiving raw PVC/ABC materials; transporting to OEM; regional hub warehousing |
| Operations | 3D design & prototyping; quality control (QC) |
| Outbound Logistics | Blind box packaging; omnichannel distribution network (robo-shops, retail stores, online platforms, cross-border logistics) |
| Marketing & Sales | Gamified sales channels; co-branding & strategic licensing; experiential marketing |
| Service | Secondary market & community building; limited edition support |
| Firm Infrastructure | Global expansion operations; IP portfolio management |
| Human Resource Management | Talent scouting; artist relations |
| Technology Development | Customer data analytics; digital commerce tech |
| Procurement (strategic sourcing) | IP acquisition & artist incubators; downstream OEM sourcing; raw material sourcing |

Note how the same firm's *artist relationship* splits three ways: **acquiring** the IP
and the licensing deal is **Procurement**, **managing** the artist as talent is **HRM**,
and **promoting** the collaboration is **Marketing & Sales**.

## Building and using your own value chain

Three steps:

1. **Map out** the sub-activities that create value for all primary and support activities.
2. **Analyse** the sub-activities to make sure each provides **more value than its cost**.
3. **Examine and optimise the linkages** among independent activities.

The deck adds an important caveat: Porter's value chain was designed for
**manufacturing-heavy companies in the 1980s**, but today value creation is often
digital, decentralised or service-based — so **not all companies need all five primary
activities**, and some execute them through partners or platforms.

Why bother at all? The deck lists four payoffs: **understand how value is created**,
**identify innovation opportunities** (where can we reduce cost, improve speed or
enhance customer experience?), **drive strategic innovation** (not just in product, but
in process, service, technology or sustainability), and **enable cross-functional
collaboration** across IT, marketing and service.

The in-class activity is the exam question in disguise: map the value chain of a
product, identifying **at least 5 primary + 2 support activities**, then name the
competitive advantages, top values, key inefficiencies and next steps.
