## Build the team on complementary capability

Some student teams struggle because the members share interests but lack the skills the project needs. Shared interests unite a
team, but complementary capabilities make it succeed. `[slides 3-4]`

When choosing members, look at capability (what skills they bring), commitment (whether they will complete their responsibilities),
collaboration (whether they can listen and work with others) and learning mindset (whether they will develop new skills). `[slide 5]`

Map the capability gaps before you start. In the CampusCup example, four members rate across research, design, technical and business
skills. The gaps show which capability the team must strengthen. `[slide 6]`

## Avoid groupthink

Groupthink looks like agreement but is avoidance. In the example, one member pushes their idea, another says "I don't mind," a third
says "everyone agrees, so I agree," and a fourth has another idea but does not want to argue. The question is whether the team agrees or
simply avoids disagreeing. `[slide 7]`

A fair way to select an idea runs in four steps: **propose** (share ideas), **discuss** (examine concerns), **decide** (choose fairly, with
the method agreed in advance) and **commit** (support the choice). Evaluate feasibility, user needs and team capability. `[slide 8]`

## How teams develop

Tuckman's model describes five stages. **Forming** is when the team meets and sets goals, and roles are unclear. **Storming** is conflict
over ideas and direction. **Norming** is when shared rules and trust form. **Performing** is high collaboration and results. **Adjourning**
is reflection, closure and celebration. Disagreement is a normal part of teamwork and usually signals the storming stage. `[slides 9-10]`

> **Exam focus.** A scenario describing disagreement over the MVP features is **storming**, not a failure of the team. Norming comes after
> the team has agreed how it will decide.

## Working styles

DISC is a simple way to talk about working styles. **D** (dominance) is bold and decisive, with the risk of impatience under stress.
**I** (influence) is outgoing and persuasive, and can over-talk under stress. **S** (steadiness) is calm and cooperative, and avoids conflict
under stress. **C** (conscientious) is analytical and precise, and can become overcritical. `[slide 11]`

A startup team blends D and I energy with S and C discipline. D and I types suit vision and drive (founder, product visionary, marketing lead).
S and C types suit structure and delivery (operations, finance, QA). Balanced types suit support and learning (design, analysis, HR).
`[slide 12]`

Agree on three working rules before starting: participation (everyone contributes and can raise concerns), responsibility (members say early if
they cannot deliver), and respect (challenge ideas, not people). `[slide 13]`

## Conflict has three types

Task conflict is about the work: which features to build. Process conflict is about how the work runs: who conducts user testing. Relationship
conflict is personal: "you never listen to me." Disagreement about a task can improve innovation. Personal conflict damages trust. `[slide 14]`

The five ways to handle conflict are **competing** (act decisively), **collaborating** (find a solution that satisfies both), **compromising**
(each side gives something up), **avoiding** (postpone or step away) and **accommodating** (put the other party's concern first). `[slide 15]`

When a conversation gets difficult, use **Fact → Impact → Request**. State the fact, what it caused, and what you need next. Avoid
"you are always irresponsible." Focus on the problem and the next action, not blame. `[slide 16]`

## Negotiate on interests, not positions

In the CampusCup example, one student's position is "we need five features" and the other's is "we should build only two." The interests
behind them are to impress potential users and to launch on time. Understand the interest before deciding what to give up. `[slide 17]`

The win-win technique is the conditional offer: "If you can…, then we can…" In the café example, the café cannot assign staff to cup returns,
and the team offers to manage a collection point during the pilot if the café allows one. This creates value for both sides rather than
splitting concessions. `[slide 18]`

The CampusCup pilot shows the result: a one-week pilot in off-peak hours, students manage registration and returns, and the café owner gives
feedback afterwards. Ask what each side gains and what remains unresolved. `[slide 19]`

> **Exam focus.** The win-win is the "if you can…, then we can…" exchange. A compromise that splits the difference does not create new value,
> so it is the weaker answer when the question offers both.

## Stakeholders have different concerns

Stakeholder engagement starts with what matters to each party. Students want convenience and simplicity. The café owner wants little time and
workload. The university wants safety, rules and environmental impact. The innovation team wants to test and learn. `[slide 20]`

Resistance is information. "It takes too much time" can be met with a smaller pilot. "It might create problems" with demonstrated safeguards.
"I don't see the benefit" with evidence or user feedback. "Students won't use it" with a test on a small group first. `[slide 21]`

Commitment comes when stakeholders' concerns shape the solution. The deck's sequence is listen, adapt, demonstrate, agree. In the university
example, the team listens to sustainability concerns, adjusts the pilot to measure disposable cups avoided, demonstrates the collection
process, and agrees responsibilities and a review date. `[slide 22]`

## Group activity: a lost-and-found pilot

A student team proposes a QR-based lost-and-found service, to test for two weeks in one building. Each stakeholder wants something different.
The team wants to launch within two weeks, gain users and collect feedback. The university wants better student services at minimum cost and legal
risk. Security and building staff want no extra workload and no fraudulent claims. Students want quick recovery, privacy and an easy system.
`[slides 23-24]`

The negotiation has to answer five questions: who manages the reports, how ownership is verified, who is responsible if an item goes missing or
is claimed wrongly, what student information is protected, and how success is measured. The deliverable is a short agreement covering the pilot
scope, stakeholder responsibilities, safeguards and success indicators. `[slides 25-26]`

## Wrap-up

Build the right team on shared goals, complementary skills and commitment. Encourage different opinions to avoid groupthink. Resolve conflict
on the issue rather than the person. Negotiate on interests to find trade-offs both sides accept. Engage stakeholders by listening to their
concerns and gaining their commitment. `[slide 26]`
