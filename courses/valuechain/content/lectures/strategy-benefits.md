## Delivery success is not business success

A project can be delivered on time, within budget, with all deliverables accepted, and still create little value. The deck's
comparison makes the point: a project that was one month late and 10% over budget but drives high adoption and significant
operational improvement creates more value than an on-time project nobody uses. `[slides 4-5]`

Monitoring asks whether the project is delivered as approved. Strategy asks whether the project should exist, and whether it
still should. Effective management connects delivery performance to strategic value. `[slide 5]`

## Strategic alignment

**Strategic alignment** is the extent to which a project supports the organisation's strategic objectives and priorities. The
questions are why the organisation needs the project, which objective it supports, what measurable improvement it should
create, and whether it is still relevant. `[slide 6]`

Without alignment, resources go to low-priority projects, projects conflict, and innovation creates little business value.
With it, resources focus on key priorities and projects deliver measurable value. `[slide 7]`

Alignment works at three levels. **Corporate** sets the direction (for example, improve operational efficiency). **Portfolio**
decides which initiatives deserve investment (inventory, logistics, digital sales). **Project** implements the chosen
initiative (an inventory system deployment). `[slide 8]`

The FreshMart chain shows how to link strategy to a project. The strategy is to improve retail operational efficiency. The
strategic objective is to reduce inventory inaccuracies and shortages. The initiative is to modernise inventory management. The
project is the inventory management system, with the expected outcome of accurate, timely stock information and the expected
benefit of reduced losses and better availability. `[slide 9]`

## Screening: not every good idea is the right investment

A good idea can still be the wrong investment. Screening looks at strategic contribution, expected business value, feasibility
and organisational readiness, risk and uncertainty, and investment and resource requirements. FreshMart's three candidates
(inventory accuracy, AI demand forecasting and a customer loyalty app) are scored against these criteria. `[slides 10-11]`

Alignment is not set once at initiation. Organisations change, projects produce new information, and stakeholders' priorities
move. Strategy guides projects, projects inform strategy, and insights from data, the market and performance feed back in.
Management must review the strategic relevance periodically. `[slides 12-13]`

## When to reconsider a project

The deck's FreshMart case is 60% complete when a competitor launches much better fulfilment services, customer expectations
shift toward faster delivery, the estimated remaining cost rises, and the expected benefit from the original solution declines.
Each of those is a reason to reconsider. `[slide 14]`

Management then chooses one of four decisions. **Continue** when the remaining benefits still justify the investment.
**Modify** when changes could improve future value. **Pause** when an important uncertainty needs investigation. **Terminate**
when the expected remaining value no longer justifies the remaining cost and risk. `[slide 15]`

> **Exam focus.** Prior spending is a **sunk cost**. Decide on the remaining benefits against the remaining cost and risk,
> not on what has already been spent.

## Outputs, outcomes and benefits

**Benefits Realization Management (BRM)** is the process of planning, delivering, measuring and sustaining the benefits a project
is expected to produce. It asks five questions: what value is expected, how the project enables it, how it will be measured,
when it should be realised, and who is accountable. `[slides 16-17]`

An **output** is the product or capability the project delivers. An **outcome** is the operational change from using it. A
**benefit** is the measurable positive value. In the FreshMart example: the inventory system is the output, more accurate stock
information is the outcome, and lower cost of inventory errors is the benefit. `[slide 17]`

A **benefits map** explains how the deliverables are expected to create value. `[slide 18]`

Each benefit needs a KPI, a baseline and a target. Inventory accuracy moves from 88% to at least 97%, the stockout rate from 8%
to at most 4%, manual reconciliation from 4 hours to at most 1 hour, and active user adoption from 0% to at least 90%. `[slide 19]`

Measuring benefits means comparing before and after, and then answering three questions: has the target been achieved, is the
improvement sustainable, and can we show that the project caused it. `[slide 20]`

Projects can finish before their benefits are realised. The lifecycle runs from delivering the system during execution, to
handover, to users adopting new processes, to benefits measured and sustained in operations. `[slide 21]`

Five roles share the work. The **sponsor** maintains the strategic justification. The **project manager** delivers the agreed
outputs. The **benefit owner** achieves the expected business benefits. The **operations team** adopts and sustains new processes.
The **analyst** measures benefits and reports results. `[slide 22]`

A **benefits realisation plan** turns expectations into commitments: each benefit has a measure, a target, an owner and a review
rhythm. Stock accuracy of at least 97% is reviewed monthly by the operations manager, and system adoption of at least 90% weekly
during rollout by the business process owner. `[slide 23]`

## Innovation is a managed experiment

Projects are innovation engines. They translate ideas into practical solutions, test new technologies under controlled conditions,
generate evidence about feasibility and value, build capability, and enable successful innovations to scale. `[slide 24]`

**Innovation management** is the systematic process of developing, selecting, implementing and scaling ideas that create value.
It identifies problems, generates and evaluates solutions, tests feasibility, implements validated innovations, and monitors value.
`[slide 25]`

Four types of innovation are named: **product or service** (a smart retail application), **process** (automated inventory
replenishment), **business model** (a subscription delivery service) and **organisational** (cross-functional agile teams).
`[slide 26]`

Innovation involves uncertainty, so critical assumptions are validated before scaling. The **stage-gate** approach moves through
Idea (is there a real problem?), Concept (is the proposed solution attractive?), Prototype (can the solution work?), Pilot (does it
produce useful results in practice?), and Scale (can the benefits be reproduced more widely?). At each gate the team continues,
revises, pauses or stops. `[slide 27]`

> **Exam focus.** Match the stage to its question. A prototype that cannot work as designed fails at the **Prototype** gate,
> because that gate asks whether the solution can work at all.

An **innovation ecosystem** is a network of organisations and stakeholders that collaborate to develop, support and commercialise
innovations. Thailand's AI ecosystem is the deck's case. `[slides 28-29]`

## Measuring alignment and innovation

Five measurement areas track whether the portfolio is working. Strategic alignment asks what share of investment is linked to
strategic objectives. Innovation outputs ask what new capabilities were created. Innovation adoption asks what share of target
users use the solution. Innovation benefits ask what savings or revenue come from it. Ecosystem collaboration asks whether partners
produce results. Organisational learning asks whether lessons from one initiative are applied in the next. `[slide 30]`

Apple is the deck's ecosystem case. Its strategy of an integrated user experience across devices and services leads to
interoperable products and easier transitions, which in turn support customer retention and service revenue. `[slide 31]`

## Wrap-up

Strategic alignment connects projects to organisational objectives. Effective delivery does not guarantee business benefits.
Benefits need measurable outcomes and clear ownership. Innovation has to move beyond ideas to adoption and value. Strategy should be
reviewed using the evidence projects produce. `[slide 33]`
