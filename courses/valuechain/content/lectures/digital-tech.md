## Digital technology is for visibility, prediction and optimisation

Digital technology helps a manager answer three questions: what is happening (visibility), what is likely to happen
(prediction), and what should we do (optimisation). The rest of the lecture is about those three questions. `[slide 4]`

## Digitization, digitalization and transformation

These three words are the most common confusion in the topic. `[slides 6-7]`

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Digitization** | converting analog information into digital data | paper inventory records into Excel or a database |
| **Digitalization** | using digital technology to improve an existing process | barcode scanning automatically updates inventory |
| **Digital transformation (DX)** | redesigning how the organisation creates and delivers value | integrated ERP, IoT and analytics that enable real-time replenishment |

Digitization changes the record. Digitalization changes a step in a process. Only digital transformation changes the
business model or the value flow. The deck lists seven types of DX. `[slide 7]`

> **Exam focus.** A scenario question gives you one change and asks for the term. Ask whether the change only converts the
> record (digitization), improves a step (digitalization), or changes how value is created (transformation).

## The value creation flow

The lecture uses a single chain: **DATA → INFORMATION → INSIGHT → DECISION → ACTION**. The example is 1,250 daily orders
(data), an 18% decline (information), a decline concentrated in Bangkok stores (insight), an investigation of store-level
availability (decision), and an adjustment to replenishment (action). `[slide 9]`

Digital systems add value at each step, but only if the decision and action follow. A dashboard that stops at information
has not yet changed anything.

## The four levels of analytics

Each level answers a different question. `[slides 10-11]`

| Level | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive** | What happened? | sales decreased 12% |
| **Diagnostic** | Why did it happen? | stockouts increased |
| **Predictive** | What is likely to happen? | demand forecast |
| **Prescriptive** | What should we do? | recommended inventory level |

> **Exam focus.** Match the question first, then the level. "Why" is diagnostic, "what will" is predictive, and "what should we
> do" is prescriptive. Descriptive analytics is the only level that reports a past result without explaining it.

Analytics appears in both operations and supply chain management, where it supports visibility of the supply chain. `[slides 12-14]`

## Project data and early warning signs

A project produces data across its life cycle: scope, schedule, resources, cost, risk, quality and progress. A digital PM
system brings these into dashboards and alerts. The questions it answers are we on schedule, are we within budget, are
resources overloaded, which risks need attention, and is quality acceptable. `[slide 15]`

Leading indicators give early warning: unresolved issues rising, milestones repeatedly slipping, resource overload, and
more change requests. The conceptual chain is project data → signal → warning → management action. `[slide 16]`

The manager's view moves from report (what happened) to dashboard (what needs attention) to analytics (why, and what
next) to decision (what to do). `[slide 17]`

## Machine learning and generative AI

These are different tools. Machine learning mainly learns patterns from historical data: demand prediction, failure
prediction, delivery-delay prediction, customer churn, and fraud or anomaly detection. Generative AI mainly generates or
interprets content: summarising project documents, drafting risk registers, analysing customer comments, supporting
scenario generation, and extracting information from reports. `[slide 18]`

A **digital twin** is a digital representation of a physical system or process that uses data to understand, simulate or
predict its behaviour. A digital twin is not just a 3D model or a dashboard. `[slide 19]`

**Decision support** recommends an action, such as raising safety stock. **Automation** acts on its own, such as placing a
replenishment order when inventory falls below a threshold. Automate processes that are stable, rule-based and worth it.
`[slides 20, 26]`

## Trustworthy digital management

Digital systems create value only when the data and systems can be trusted. That needs data quality, data governance and
cybersecurity. `[slide 21]`

**Data quality** has six dimensions. **Accuracy** means the data reflects reality. **Completeness** means all required data
is present. **Consistency** means data is aligned across systems and over time. **Timeliness** means it is available when
needed. **Validity** means it follows defined formats, rules and business logic. **Uniqueness** means each record is
distinct, with no unnecessary duplication. The deck's warning: bad data plus advanced analytics gives bad decisions, faster.
`[slide 22]`

**Data governance** asks who owns the data, who can access it, who defines it and who is responsible for its quality. The
ethical issues are privacy, bias, transparency and AI accountability. `[slide 23]`

A highly connected supply chain creates more digital dependencies and more attack surfaces, so cybersecurity is a trade-off
against connectivity. `[slide 24]`

Digital risk falls into four types. **Data risk** is incorrect or incomplete data. **System risk** is downtime or
integration failure. **Cyber risk** is unauthorised access or attack. **AI risk** is bias, hallucination or inappropriate
automation. The management response is controls, governance and human oversight. `[slide 25]`

## Start with the business problem

The lecture closes with a rule: start with the business problem, not the technology. A digital tool that is not tied to a
specific operational question will not change the decision. `[slide 26]`
