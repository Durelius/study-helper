## Execution is people, process and technology

The deck defines project execution as people plus process plus technology. Before execution starts, the project should have
answered why the project exists (business case), what it aims to achieve (charter), what is included (scope), what work is
required (WBS), when it will happen (schedule), who and what is needed (resources), what it will cost (budget), what might
go wrong (risk register), how the team will coordinate (communication plan), and what "good" means (quality plan).
`[slides 5-6]`

Execution then meets reality. Customer needs change, activities take longer, staff become unavailable, new risks appear,
technical problems emerge and priorities shift. The project manager's job is to respond to these, not to pretend the plan
still holds. `[slides 7-8]`

## Predictive or agile: match the approach to the uncertainty

Predictive delivery plans first, executes and delivers. It suits high certainty: stable requirements, known technology, low
change and a predictable deliverable. Agile plans, builds, learns and reviews in repeated cycles. It suits high uncertainty:
evolving requirements, high innovation and valuable feedback. `[slide 9]`

| | Predictive | Agile |
|---|---|---|
| Requirements | defined early | evolve |
| Planning | upfront | progressive |
| Delivery | one major deliverable | frequent increments |
| Change | controlled | expected |
| Customer involvement | at milestones | continuous |
| Success measured by | plan conformance | value and learning |

`[slide 10]`

> **Exam focus.** Agile does not eliminate planning. It changes how often planning happens. A question that says
> "requirements are evolving" points to agile, and "requirements are fixed and well understood" points to predictive.

The problem agile solves is a long planning cycle followed by a long development cycle, where the customer sees the output
only at the end, and requirements have already changed. The agile response is a small plan, a small delivery, feedback,
adjustment and the next delivery. The aim is to shorten the time between doing the work and learning whether it creates value.
`[slide 11]`

Agile is adaptive (it responds to new information), iterative (it improves through repeated cycles) and customer-focused (it
delivers useful value frequently). It is a mindset, not a single tool or framework. `[slide 12]`

## Scrum, Kanban and how they relate

Agile is the mindset. **Scrum** is one framework for how to plan and execute. **Kanban** is a visual system for tracking and
improving flow. Kanban on its own adds visibility and control but not adaptability. Kanban with agile adds flexibility and
flow. `[slides 14, 21]`

A **sprint** is a short, time-boxed period, usually two to four weeks, in which the team completes a set amount of work.
Sprint planning sets the length of the sprint, the sprint goal, and where the team starts. `[slide 15]`

Scrum has three accountabilities: the product owner, the scrum master and the development team, each with a distinct job.
`[slides 16-17]`

Three artifacts make the work visible. The **product backlog** is the prioritised list of all work the product may need.
The **sprint backlog** is the work selected for the current sprint. The **increment** is the working, tested result produced
by the sprint. `[slide 18]`

The **daily scrum** is a 15-minute coordination meeting. It asks where the team is relative to the sprint goal, what has
changed, what is blocking progress, and what to adjust today. It supports self-management and quick problem-solving.
`[slide 19]`

The **sprint review** improves the product. The team and stakeholders ask what was delivered, what feedback was received
and what should happen next. The **retrospective** improves the way the team works, asking what worked, what did not, and
what the team should change. `[slide 20]`

## Kanban and work in progress

Kanban rests on three principles: visualise the workflow, limit work in progress (WIP), and improve flow. `[slide 22]`

Without WIP limits, everyone starts something, too much work sits unfinished, queues grow, context switching increases and
the cycle time lengthens. With WIP limits, the team finishes existing work, frees capacity, pulls the next item and the flow
speeds up. `[slide 23]`

> **Exam focus.** WIP limits are about flow, not effort. The answer that says "start more work to keep people busy" is
> the trap.

## Measuring progress during execution

A **burndown chart** tracks the remaining work against time. The x-axis is time in the sprint, and the y-axis is story points
or tasks remaining. It answers one question: are we likely to finish the sprint work? `[slides 24-25]`

Reading it: a line above the ideal means the team may be behind, a line below the ideal means it may be ahead, a flat line
means no work is being completed, and a line that rises means work has been added. `[slide 24]`

The burndown is a team-level measure. It is not the same as project control. Sprint-level tools (burndown, Kanban flow,
velocity) answer whether this team can complete the sprint. Project-level tools (schedule variance, cost variance,
milestones, KPIs, earned value) answer whether the overall project is performing to plan. `[slide 26]`

## Leading during execution

The leader's role shifts during execution from commanding to enabling. Good execution needs visibility: everyone should be
able to see the same picture. `[slides 27-29]`

## Digital tools support, but do not decide

A tool can show tasks, schedule, dependencies, a Kanban board, documents, status and dashboards. It cannot decide what matters
most, why priorities changed, whether quality is acceptable, how to resolve a conflict, or which trade-off to make. `[slide 31]`

The common project tools are task lists, milestones, Gantt charts, boards for progress stages, calendars, status updates, live
chat, time tracking, shared notes and dashboards. Reports turn the data into insight. `[slides 32-34]`

The deck's ClickUp example shows an agile workflow: create a space, add tasks to a backlog, organise them into a sprint board
(To Do, In Progress, Done), then track sprint progress with a burndown chart. `[slides 35, 42-43]`

The review rhythm matters as much as the tool. A daily spot check takes 10-30 minutes at the end of each day, a weekly
roundup feeds a short report to management, a monthly review informs resource allocation and scope, and a quarterly analysis
changes time estimates and planning. `[slide 49]`

## Applying it to a group project

For a class group project, assign the Scrum roles (product owner, scrum master, development team), use a Kanban board to
visualise the tasks, track progress with a burndown chart, and manage deadlines in the tool. `[slide 50]`
