## Creating value is not the same as sustaining it

A successful project creates measurable benefits, but those benefits have to last. Environmental impact, supply disruption and
operating cost can erode long-term value, so sustainability and resilience belong in every investment decision. The deck's
question is how a company can create value without unacceptable long-term costs or risks. `[slides 5]`

## Sustainability has three dimensions

A sustainable value chain balances three dimensions. **Economic** is financial viability and operational efficiency.
**Environmental** is resource use, emissions and waste. **Social** is worker safety, fair practices and community impact.
`[slide 6]`

The impacts run along the whole chain: sourcing (where materials come from), production (energy and material used),
transportation (fuel and resources), consumption (how efficiently the product is used) and end-of-life (what happens when it is no
longer needed). `[slide 7]`

Sustainability decisions involve trade-offs. An environmentally better alternative may cost more up front. A cheap solution may
create waste or operational risk. Reducing one impact may increase another. So evaluate the whole chain, not one activity.
`[slide 8]`

The restaurant case makes this concrete. Switching to takeaway packaging that costs ฿6 a box instead of ฿3, on 10,000 orders a
month, adds ฿30,000 a month. The business has to check whether it can recover that cost, whether the environmental benefit is
real and verified, and whether customers will pay for it. The deck suggests testing customer acceptance before switching.
`[slide 9]`

> **Exam focus.** The extra cost of an alternative is the difference between the two totals: here ฿60,000 minus ฿30,000 gives
> ฿30,000 a month. The total new cost is not the increase.

## Resilience: a disruption spreads

One disruption can spread across several activities. Small problems can create large downstream costs, so supply chain decisions
should consider the dependencies and the recovery options. `[slide 10]`

Efficiency and resilience pull in different directions. Efficiency chooses the lowest purchase price, minimum inventory, a single
supplier and the cheapest transport. Resilience chooses reliable supply, appropriate safety stock, alternative suppliers and backup
delivery. Efficiency reduces normal cost. Resilience reduces vulnerability. Managers must balance both. `[slide 11]`

The café case: Supplier A charges ฿300 a kg and Supplier B ฿330 a kg, on 100 kg a month. That is a ฿3,000 monthly difference.
Supplier A is sometimes late and Supplier B is more reliable. The questions are how much a shortage would cost the café, whether
the extra ฿3,000 is justified, and whether buying from both suppliers would be better. `[slide 12]`

Companies improve resilience through diversification (alternative suppliers or logistics), a reasonable safety stock,
visibility into inventory and supplier performance, contingency plans prepared before the disruption, and flexibility to change
sourcing, production or delivery. `[slide 13]`

Resilience is measured with four KPIs. **On-time delivery rate** asks whether orders arrive when promised. **Supplier
reliability** asks how often suppliers meet commitments. **Time to Recover (TTR)** asks how long operations take to restore.
**Stockout rate** asks how often customers face shortages. `[slide 14]`

The deck's gap calculation: if the supplier needs 8 days to recover and the company holds only 5 days of inventory, there is a
3-day gap to cover. `[slide 14]`

## Circular economy: keep resources in use

A linear economy takes, makes, uses and disposes. A **circular economy** is designed to reduce waste and keep products,
components and materials useful for as long as possible. The strategies are reduce material use, reuse, repair, refurbish,
remanufacture and recycle. The objective is to minimise waste, improve resource efficiency and retain economic value. `[slides 15-16]`

Three terms are often confused. **Upcycling** turns the recovered material into something of higher value. **Downcycling** uses it
for a lower-grade purpose. **Recycling** recovers and processes the material without necessarily raising or lowering its value.
`[slide 17]`

Higher product value does not automatically mean higher profit. Processing costs, labour and market demand still matter. The
refurbished-laptop case shows this: the refurbished laptop and the new laptop both give ฿2,500 contribution per unit, but the
refurbished one carries more risk of unexpected repair costs, and the new one needs more money to buy inventory. Refurbishing does
not automatically have the lower environmental impact either. `[slides 17-18]`

Reuse becomes cheaper only after enough uses. A disposable container costs ฿8 per use. A reusable one costs ฿80 to buy plus ฿5 per use
for cleaning and handling, so its cost per use is 80 / N + 5 for N successful uses. It falls below ฿8 only once N passes about 27.
`[slide 19]`

Circular projects fail for predictable reasons: collection or processing costs run higher than expected, products cannot be reused
as many times as planned, customers do not return them, quality is inconsistent, and demand does not cover operating costs.
`[slide 20]`

Evaluate total cost rather than the purchase price. That means the initial investment, operating and maintenance cost, transport and
handling, repair, cleaning or processing, and end-of-life cost net of any recovery value. `[slide 21]`

## Sustainable innovation needs collaboration

A company rarely controls the whole chain. Suppliers influence materials, quality and reliability. Logistics partners affect
transport. Customers influence adoption and returns. Technology and recovery partners bring specialised capability. `[slide 22]`

Digital technology supports sustainability in specific ways. AI forecasting reduces overstocking and stockouts, IoT sensors monitor
temperature and energy use, inventory systems improve visibility of materials and stock, and route optimisation cuts unnecessary
transport. Technology should address a specific operational problem, performance must be measured afterwards, and staff must be able
and willing to use it. `[slide 23]`

## Show that a project creates value

Output is what was implemented. Outcome is what changed in operations. Benefit is the measurable improvement. A food waste project
delivers a new demand forecasting tool (output), better ordering decisions (outcome), and lower food waste and purchasing losses
(benefit), measured by food waste cost per month. `[slide 24]`

## Group workshop: choose one investment

A university with a limited budget can choose one of three proposals: solar panels to reduce purchased electricity, a smart inventory
system to cut cafeteria food waste, or water refill stations to reduce bottled water. Score each proposal on financial feasibility,
environmental benefit, operational feasibility, risk and resilience, and measurable value. The group task is to select one, justify
it, name two KPIs and state one assumption to test before a large investment. `[slides 25-27]`

## Key takeaways

Sustainability balances economic, environmental and social impacts. Resilience prepares for disruption while managing cost. Circular
reuse and recycling create value but are not automatically profitable. Innovation tests assumptions before scaling. Project success is
measured by real benefits, not by completed deliverables. `[slide 28]`
