## Completed is not accepted, and accepted is not closed

These three words are the core trap of closing. `[slide 5]`

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Completed** | the work has been finished | mobile app developed |
| **Accepted** | an authorised stakeholder approves the deliverable | client approves the app after testing |
| **Closed** | the formal closing activities are finished | handover and project records finalised |

A project can be completed but not accepted, and accepted but not yet closed. Only closure ends the project formally.

## What closing is for

**Project closing** is the process of formally completing or terminating a project or phase. It ensures that
deliverables and acceptance are verified, responsibilities are handed over, outstanding obligations are
addressed, and performance and lessons are documented. `[slide 4]`

Not every project ends the same way. The right action depends on the situation. `[slide 6]`

| Situation | Appropriate action |
|---|---|
| All agreed work accepted | formally close the project |
| Pilot or phase completed | evaluate and close the phase, if appropriate |
| Project no longer justified | authorise termination and document closure |
| Work still in progress | report progress and plan remaining activities |
| Scope or topic changed | document and assess the change; continue under the approved direction |

> **Exam focus.** The closing scenario questions are classification questions. Ask whether the work is
> accepted, whether the project is still justified, and whether the scope changed. The answer follows from
> those three facts.

## The closing process

The deck lays out closing as a sequence of four steps. `[slide 7]`

1. **Verify deliverables.** Compare planned, completed and accepted work.
2. **Obtain acceptance.** Confirm acceptance criteria and approval.
3. **Transfer ownership and close obligations.** Hand over, and settle administrative matters.
4. **Evaluate and learn.** Assess performance, record lessons, and archive.

Before closing, check which deliverables still need action. A deliverable can be completed and still not
accepted, such as a two-week pilot that is complete but pending acceptance. `[slide 8]`

## Acceptance criteria

**Acceptance criteria** define the conditions a deliverable must satisfy. Each deliverable needs its own
criterion, so "done" can be checked. In the digital ordering example, the ordering system must process test
orders correctly, the payment function must record transactions accurately, the user interface must pass the
agreed usability test, and data management must meet the agreed privacy and access requirements. `[slide 9]`

## Unfinished work is never silently dropped

Before closing, every outstanding item must be addressed or formally transferred, with an owner and a next
action. Unfinished work cannot simply disappear from the project records. `[slide 10]`

## Handover: who takes responsibility

A completed product still needs someone to operate and maintain it. A handover should settle who owns the
product after the project, who provides maintenance and support, what training, documents and access are
needed, and which risks or unresolved issues are passed on. `[slide 11]`

## Administrative closure

Closure may also require confirming contract and payment obligations, releasing resources and equipment,
closing or transferring unresolved issues, archiving plans, deliverables and records, and revoking
unnecessary system access. `[slide 12]`

## Delivery success is not business success

The deck separates three levels of result. `[slides 13-14]`

| Level | Example (inventory system) |
|---|---|
| **Output** | the inventory management system is delivered |
| **Outcome** | inventory accuracy improves |
| **Benefit** | inventory losses decline |

A project can deliver its output perfectly and still fail to produce the benefit. Evaluation has to look past
the output.

## Evaluate against KPIs, then decide

Evaluation compares each KPI's target with its actual value. The café pilot example shows the pattern: cups
issued hit the target (200 against 200), but the cup return rate fell short (75% against a target of at least
90%), the handling time missed (4 minutes against at most 3), and customer satisfaction beat its target (85%
against at least 80%). `[slide 15]`

After evaluation, management chooses one of four decisions. `[slide 16]`

| Decision | When appropriate |
|---|---|
| **Scale** | benefits and feasibility are sufficiently validated |
| **Improve** | the solution is promising but performance gaps remain |
| **Extend testing** | important assumptions are still uncertain |
| **Stop** | expected value no longer justifies further investment |

## Lessons learned

**Lessons learned** are insights from experience that improve future performance. The deck asks four questions:
what did we expect, what actually happened, why was there a difference, and what should we do differently.
Lessons must be collected **throughout the project**, not only at closure. `[slide 17]`

The café example shows the chain from problem to action. The pilot started a week late because café approval
was requested too late. The lesson is that stakeholder approval must be planned as a project dependency.
The improvement is an approval milestone with a named owner on the Gantt chart. `[slide 18]`

## Knowledge management

Useful project knowledge includes plans and approved changes, technical and process documents, testing results
and feedback, and the lessons, decisions and unresolved issues. Apply three principles: **capture** important
knowledge, **store** it somewhere accessible and secure, and **reuse** it in future projects. `[slides 19-20]`

## Final project presentation

Each team presents the planned, achieved and learned, in **at most 4 minutes**, focusing on actual progress and
evidence rather than the original plan. `[slide 22]`

Use this timing plan. `[slide 23]`

| Time | What to present |
|---|---|
| 30 sec | **Project overview**: the problem and the objective |
| 60 sec | **Project status and deliverables**: what is completed, tested or unfinished, with evidence |
| 60 sec | **PM performance**: the Gantt chart, responsibilities, and major changes or challenges |
| 45 sec | **Stakeholders and value**: whose needs are met, and what feedback you received |
| 45 sec | **Lessons learned and next steps**: what you would improve, and what happens next |

The timing adds up to 4 minutes. Weight the presentation toward status, evidence and PM performance, because
those are where the deck says marks are won.

> **Exam focus.** A project brief review asks for the same content as this presentation: the problem, the status
> with evidence, how the project was managed, the stakeholders served, and the lessons. Structure your answer
> in this order.

## Team accountability

Outcomes are collective, but responsibilities must be traceable. Every team member should be able to explain
their assigned tasks, the progress they made, the challenges they met and how they handled them, and the
evidence for their contribution. `[slide 21]`
