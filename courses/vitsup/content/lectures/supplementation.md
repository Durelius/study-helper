## The key definitions

| Term | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Nutrient** | a chemical compound essential for life and physiological function | protein, vitamin C, iron |
| **Supplement** | a product that **adds nutrients** to the diet, **complementing** food | multivitamin pill, protein powder |
| **Functional food** | a food with **health benefits beyond basic nutrition** | probiotic yoghurt, green tea (antioxidant), oats (cholesterol) |
| **Superfood** | a nutrient-rich food, but **mostly a marketing term** | acai, kale |
| **Nutraceutical** | a **bioactive compound** with health benefits that **needs clinical evidence** | curcumin phytosome, pro/prebiotics, food for special medical uses |

Functional foods are **plant-based or animal-based** (mostly fish and probiotics), and
natural, modified, enriched or improved. The good fish is **sardines**; **tuna** is high in
the food chain and carries **heavy metals**.

**Nutraceuticals vs pharmaceuticals.**
- A nutraceutical sits on a **spectrum from food to a regulatory grey area**, with benefits
  from essential to targeted to therapeutic.
- It does require a clinical trial, "HOWEVER sometimes only about 10 people", so **check the
  methodology** (ConsumerLab is independent).
- Regulation controls the **ingredient rather than the product** (Cedric's notes), so about
  70% of products are "hand waving".
- **Formulation** is part of the distinction: plain turmeric extract is **1% bioavailable**,
  and liposome or phytosome forms reach about **50×**.

> **Exam focus.** Expect "which term fits this product?" questions. A pill that adds
> nutrients is a **supplement**. A food with benefits beyond nutrition is a **functional
> food**. A bioactive backed by trials is a **nutraceutical**. "Superfood" is a
> **marketing** word.

## Why the market is booming

- **Post-COVID health awareness** and **ultra-processed diets** push younger people to
  supplements.
- There is a **shift from patient to consumer**: people make proactive health decisions and
  own their health, and traditional herbs have moved into pills, powders and functional drinks.
- **Asia's supplement industry exceeds USD 40 billion.**
  - China and India are growing fastest.
  - **Japan** has the longest history of functional foods (Yakult) and advanced regulation.
  - ASEAN is emerging.
- The top drivers are **immunity**, **beauty and skin**, **weight management**,
  **longevity and healthy ageing**, the **ageing population**, and **mental and cognitive function**.
- Consumers love **multi-benefit** products. Redoxon (vitamin C + D + zinc) leads immunity
  in Singapore, and **halal** certification matters in Indonesia and Malaysia.
- **Precision nutrition**, meaning formulas for one person, is where the business is going.

## Benefits and risks

**Benefits** (from class):
- filling nutrient gaps;
- weight management (a healthy diet, Ozempic; chitosan claims);
- cognition (omega-3, GABA);
- **athletic performance (creatine)**;
- heart and vision;
- immunity;
- sleep (**magnesium**, melatonin, glycine, GABA);
- **pregnancy nutrition (ginger for nausea)**;
- feeling better about your health.

**Risks:**
- **toxicity** if misused (fat-soluble vitamins, B6);
- **liver damage** and GI issues;
- **drug interactions**: herbs can change liver enzymes and shift another drug's
  concentration (e.g. diabetes medication), so see a doctor first;
- **heavy metals** and undeclared drugs in poorly made or uncertified products.

**Chitosan** "sucks fat out of the stomach", but it binds the fat that carries **fat-soluble
vitamins**, can trigger allergy, and slows digestion (constipation).

## How to judge a claim

1. **Is there a deficiency?** Vitamin C, D and zinc have strong evidence for **correcting
   deficiency**, not for megadosing healthy people.
2. **Is it the right nutrient?** Multivitamins are usually **underdosed**, so target
   specific gaps instead (D3, B12 for vegans, magnesium, omega-3).
3. **Is the evidence for this product or strain?** Ingredient-level claims, 10-person trials
   and "clinically tested" or "Made in Australia" badges build trust but are not proof.
4. **Is it well made?** Look for GMP and third-party testing (NSF, USP, ConsumerLab),
   reputable sellers, and regulator warnings. Watch for **copycats** that copy the pack but
   not the trials.
5. **Is the message fear or education?** COVID buying ran on fear and plausibility ahead of
   evidence. Campaigns that educate (Blackmores Immunity IQ) build durable trust.
6. **Where did you see it?** **Social media and messaging apps** are the biggest channel for
   health fraud.

Why do people still reach for pills? **Convenience**, powerful marketing, and an **illusion
of control** (quick reassurance, less guilt about lifestyle), while real lifestyle change
costs time, money and energy. The course's verdict: supplements **support health but are
not miracle cures**, and "natural" does not mean safe.
