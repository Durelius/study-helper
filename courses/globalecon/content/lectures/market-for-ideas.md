## Mokyr and useful knowledge

**Joel Mokyr**, an economic historian, received the **2025 Nobel Prize** in economics for
explaining **innovation-driven economic growth**. His main points:

- He explained it through **historical analysis**.
- **Cultural openness** is key to technological progress.
- **Useful knowledge** fuels innovation through the synergy of **propositional**
  knowledge (*why* things work, science) and **prescriptive** knowledge (*how* to
  do things, technique).
- **Supportive institutions** sustain innovation.
- He warned of threats like **protectionism stifling creative destruction**.

## The Age of Enlightenment

The **Enlightenment** was an intellectual and philosophical movement that dominated
Europe in the **17th and 18th centuries**. It happened in **Western Europe, not in China or
the Islamic world**, and it snowballed into the **Industrial Revolution**.

- It was a ferment of public debate and innovation that we might now call **"open science"**.
- Knowledge, from deep scientific insight to practical technological **know-how and
  tinkering**, became common.
- **Salons** (e.g. Madame Geoffrin's, 18th century) hosted scientific and literary
  discussion and characterised the age.

## The graph: two curves, two countries

The **market for ideas** is drawn with:

| Axis | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Vertical | **Fragmentation index**: political fragmentation, **high in Europe, low in unified China** |
| Horizontal | **Number of ideas**: useful knowledge that generates both technologically and ideologically driven growth |

Political fragmentation affects **both the demand and the supply** of ideas, which is
why there are two curves and **multiple equilibria**.

| Curve | Slope | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Size of audience** (demand) | **downward** | Fragmented Europe has a small audience in each state (each castle), which discourages innovators. Unified China could enjoy a huge audience, which in principle invites many ideas. |
| **Risk of persecution** (supply) | **upward** | As fragmentation rises, persecution becomes less effective: overlapping, poorly coordinated jurisdictions let a thinker flee across a border. Fragmented Europe lowers the risk of provocative ideas; in unified China the risk is high. |

So **fragmentation cuts both ways**: it shrinks the audience (fewer ideas) and lowers
the persecution risk (more ideas).

## Why China ended up with few ideas

On the audience curve alone, China's low fragmentation implies a **large** audience and
many ideas. But unified China sat on the **left-side equilibrium** at the bottom of the
graph (point labelled "China"): the state **imposed its preference for unity and
collective values**, and the **high risk of persecution** choked the supply of ideas.
**The low fragmentation of China, despite its large audience, results in a low number of
ideas.**

> **Exam focus.** "But why did China end up with a low number of ideas?" The answer is
> the **supply** side, persecution risk under a unified state, not a lack of audience.

## Why fragmentation alone is not enough: Africa

A fragmented state system is **never a sufficient condition** for a flourishing market
for ideas. **Africa** had *higher* political fragmentation but a *lower* number of ideas
because of:

- a **lack of intellectual consolidation** (no unified market for ideas across the
  fragments), and
- **lower payoffs relative to the cost** of producing ideas.

## What shifted Europe out

**Demand side.** Demand for intellectual work in Europe **increased** because of
**reduced access costs** (cheaper books and correspondence) and the **rise of an urban
commercial class**. This shifts the number of ideas up.

**Supply side.** Fragmented Europe had **more intellectual competition** and a **more
diverse pool of talent** to draw new ideas from.

**The Republic of Letters.** The key combination: **political fragmentation + a unified
market for ideas**. Europe's thinkers were split among many states but read and
argued with each other across borders in one transnational community, the **Republic
of Letters**. This pushed Europe into the Age of Enlightenment. It ended with **greater
levels of ideas in Europe due to open science and reduced risks of persecution**.

## Ideas as an open-source technology

- Besides access costs, incentives depended on the **expected benefits** to intellectual
  innovators.
- **Knowledge is non-rivalrous** (my using an idea does not stop you using it), so the
  market for ideas needs an institutional set-up unlike any other market: it resembles
  an **open-source technology**.
- **Open science** was the key to rapid change, because its very purpose was to
  disseminate new ideas and offer them to the marketplace.
- Contributions were disputed by the critical minds of colleagues and **rewarded with
  priority credit, not profit**.
- **The diffusion of one's ideas, not their exclusivity**, was what earned an
  innovator recognition.
