## The sommelier and the wine menu

Sommeliers now work in wine shops (**enoteca**), wine bars and duty-free kiosks, but they use
their knowledge most in restaurants. Their kit: menu, bottle opener, decanter, temperature
control, sparkling-wine opener, stopper and thermometer.

The wine menu stands for the quality of the restaurant. It should be neat and clean, in a
readable font, with space between entries, and constantly updated.

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Most popular format | **front-back** menu, for a limited selection; a big cellar uses a **photo album** |
| Order of sections | sparkling › white › rosé › red › sweet |
| Within a section | local, national, international or by country; or by style (fresh and young, aromatic, barrique) |
| One-page list | can be ordered **by price** |
| Must show | name of wine, producer, **vintage**, price **per bottle** |
| Optional | tasting notes, recommended food |

## Food menus

The menu as a list shown to customers appeared in **Paris in the late 18th century**.
Restaurants were born after the French Revolution, when the chefs of nobles lost their jobs.
Russia took up the menu under Alexander I (1810-1815), along with French table service.

The Western menu runs **Antipasti › Primi › Secondi › Dessert e Formaggi**. **Menu di Terra**
covers meat, poultry, game and vegetables; **Menu di Mare** covers seafood.

| Menu | Point |
|---|---|
| Banquet | fewer options, agreed by chef, owner and customer |
| Themed | a festival (Christmas, Valentine's), a seasonal ingredient such as truffles, or a grape, region or country |
| **Degustation** | shows off specialities; small portions; **4-5** dishes each with its wine; wines in order sparkling › white › rosé › red › sweet; **young before aged** |
| **Fix** | cuts kitchen workload; popular in tourist cities; helps sell wines that are hard to sell |

## Equipment

- **Cavatappi**: the sommelier's knife, with a two-stage lever, a corkscrew and a small
  knife. It cuts the foil (**capsula**) **below the neck**. A dedicated foil cutter is the
  **tagliacapsula**. Other openers include the air-pump needle and the two-leg (prong) opener.
- **Wine basket** and **decanter**: an aged red travels in the basket tilted slightly upward,
  is opened in the basket, and is poured slowly over a **candle** to watch for sediment.
- **Glacette**: a thermostatic bowl for sparkling, white and rosé. Also cooler jackets, ice
  buckets and thermometers.
- **Sparkling stopper**: tongs make opening easier. The stopper locks **under the neck**,
  because a used sparkling cork expands and cannot go back. Still wine takes its cork, a
  vacuum stopper, or inert-gas systems.

## Glasses

Glass blowing was invented in **Venice** in the Middle Ages and spread to Bohemia, the
Netherlands and France. **Lead crystal** began in **England** in the 18th century and was
developed by Baccarat in France. Today's shape came with Art Nouveau / Liberty. Glass is made
from silica, B2O3 and SO3 at 1,700°C, brought down to 1,400°C with sodium and potassium oxide.
It may be called **crystal above 24% PbO**; more lead gives a yellow tint.

| Wine | Glass and pour |
|---|---|
| Fresh white, rosé, fresh red | medium glass; tulip with flared rim for elegant wine, ½-⅔ full |
| Structured, soft white | wider glass, ⅔ |
| Sparkling | **flute, ⅘** so the perlage shows; the **coupe** is now for sweet sparkling |
| Medium-structured red | large glass |
| Elegant red | larger glass, ¼-⅓ |
| Very important, complex red | **oversize, 1/6-1/7** |
| Sweet wine | small glass, same shape, smaller pour |

| Brand | Country | Signature |
|---|---|---|
| **Riedel** | Austria | rounded lotus shape; stem stained black and red |
| **Zwiesel** | Germany | versatile; newest bowls wider for aeration |
| **Zalto** | Austria | handmade, very thin, expensive; Universal shape |
| **Lucaris** | Thailand | price; standard tulip; good for beginners |

## The service

1. **Cellar to table.** White and rosé go in an ice bucket (**⅔ water, ⅓ ice**) on a plate, or a glacette. An aged red goes in the basket.
2. **Presentation** from the customer's **left**, showing the label and naming the **designation, cru, vintage and producer**. Alcohol and grape only if asked. Dry the bottle first if it came from ice.
3. **Opening.** Cut the foil below the neck, turning the wrist 180°, then a vertical cut. Insert the screw at a slight angle and lift slowly with the two-step lever, quietly. Check the cork, put it on a small plate, and clean the mouth of the bottle.
4. **Sparkling.** Remove the foil and the wire with one hand over the cork. **Turn the bottle** while holding the cork against the turn, and push against the cork as it lifts. Pour quickly.
5. **Pouring.** Hold the bottom of the bottle, **thumb not in the punt**. Do not touch the rim. **Twist the wrist** to cut the drip and wipe with a cloth.
6. **Serving.** Taste for defects (change the bottle and apologise if faulty). Pour from the customer's **right**: a small taste for the person who ordered, then **clockwise, ladies first**. A bottle gives **8-10** portions. New wine means a new glass.

**Order of wines.** Lowest temperature or least residual sugar first; sweet wine last, even
though it is chilled. Sparkling: **Charmat › Crémant › Brut Blanc de Blancs › Brut Blanc de
Noirs › Millésime › Demi-sec**. At official meals (AIS rule): the Pope, Cardinals, the head
of state, president, prime minister, then ministers and ambassadors, then regional, defence,
police, province and city heads.

**Decanting** has two purposes: separating **sediment**, and **oxygenating** an aged wine.
You need a basket, decanter, tasting glass, opener, candle, matches and a small plate. Light
the candle, open in the basket, check the cork and taste, then pour slowly with the candle
under the neck. To oxygenate only, pour fast.

## Gadgets

| Gadget | How it works |
|---|---|
| **WINESAVE PRO** | sprays **argon**, inert and heavier than air, which settles on the wine; about 150 uses per can, no expiry |
| **Üllo** (2014) | captures free **sulfites** by Selective Sulfite Capture; also aerates |
| **eto** (UK) | squeezes out the air gap; keeps wine **up to 2 weeks**; "eto" means *again* in Welsh |
| **Vacu Vin** (1986) | pumps air out; the cork still has to be pulled |
| **Coravin** (2013) | a needle through the cork; **argon** replaces the wine poured; the cork reseals |

## Storage

Watch light, temperature, humidity, vibration and smells. **11-15 °C** with no swings (a
cellar 4-5 m underground), **65-70%** humidity, dim lights (no fluorescent), and no cheese or
cured meat nearby. Bottles lie **horizontal** so the cork stays wet. From the floor up:
**sparkling, white, rosé, red, important red**, because it gets slightly warmer higher up.

| Wine fridge | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Compressor** | fast; reaches target in any season; switches on and off | noise, vibration, greenhouse-gas coolant |
| **Thermoelectric** | no moving parts, no vibration, quiet, no harmful gas | **not below 10 °C** (no sparkling), runs constantly, weak above 27 °C outside (only −7 °C) |

## Wine diseases

| Disease | Bacteria | Found in | Symptom |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fioretta** | aerobic + yeast | low alcohol | white patches on the surface; odourless, tasteless |
| **Acescenza** | aerobic | low alcohol | cloudy; vinegar and acetaldehyde; sour |
| **Filante** | anaerobic | white/rosé, low sugar, alcohol and acid | viscous like oil, mucous |
| **Agrodolce** | anaerobic | high residual sugar | cloudy; ripe fruit; sweet-and-sour |
| **Girato** | anaerobic | too **low** acidity | dull, cloudy, slightly foamy, pungent; freshness gone |
| **Amarore** | anaerobic | reds kept too long | yellowish, pungent, very **bitter** |
